In the cybersecurity world, the phrase 'script kiddie' is used to describe someone who, to put it simply, doesn't know what they are doing. Think someone googling "how to find my neighbour's WiFi password" and blindly following the instructions on whatever page they land on. They might or might not succeed - at this point it is more a question of luck than of skill - but since they didn't do it themselves and don't know how to do so, most professionals in the field tend to treat them with a bit of contempt.

The rise of LLMs has unleashed the same behaviour in the field of software engineering, only at a much grander scale. There are literal hordes of individuals churning out apps and websites of unimaginable varieties - and yet they all have something missing. A button that doesn't let you click it, a textbox that's invisible on mobile but seen on a desktop, or a page that flat out doesn't do as advertised. Each time the author fixes one thing, something seemingly unrelated breaks, or the UI is fixed but the app still doesn't work. The author usually is aware of these shortcomings, but somehow, each update is never quite what they had in mind. Any experienced professional immediately recognizes these symptoms: it is a result of the author not being 'in sync' with the code - they don't know what part of the code does what, how it does it and what other parts it talks to. Traditionally, every engineer began in this exact place on day one, with zero knowledge and built up their understanding by making small changes to the code and observing the results. This took a lot of time and effort, but it was effective. Today, LLMs are taking away the need to write code, leaving those without this context in their heads to just keep trying different variations of the same prompt in the hope that something changes.

I call these individuals the prompt monkeys.

It's important to understand that everybody begins their journey as a prompt monkey. The difference between pre-LLM times to now, is that with time and practice, people used to gain a better understanding of their code and their tech, eventually pulling themselves up to a higher level of skill. If AI writes all the code for us now, how do we get better?

But this is just one of the existential questions facing freshers today. There's a ton more: How do I even get a job in the first place? If I do manage to find a job, how do I hold on to it, with AI threatening to replace me everyday? What if I don't want to be a prompt monkey forever? How do I learn and grow in this role, making a career out of it for the next few decades? Most importantly, how do I not lose myself along the way, burning out from the stress and immense workload?


### Getting a job in the first place

There's a scene from an old Indian movie that I think about a lot these days. A scientist builds a super-intelligent humanoid robot (think Terminator, but much less violent and murderous) and introduces it to his parents. When the family sits down to eat, they want to watch TV so they tell the robot to "please handle the TV". The robot, not being one to question instructions, promptly picks up the TV and throws it out the window. Problem solved, no more TV to worry about. Of course, the scientist says it wasn't the robot's fault - it did exactly as it was told! His parents should have been clear with their instructions.

While this movie predates Claude by several years, I do think its had a Simpsons moment with how well it captures the challenges we have with AI today. If you want to build an app using AI, but still need it to be better and more polished than the half-baked attempts that are the hallmark of a prompt monkey running wild, you need to very carefully, and very precisely, elucidate exactly how the system is to behave in every scenario, starting from the initial state describing what inputs you need from the user, to describing how those inputs are to be processed, what needs to be derived and how it should be done, how these steps interact with each other and what final outputs are to be shown back to the user.

In other words, we need to define an algorithm for the system. And any language that allows us to do so in precise, unambigous terms, is also known as a programming language :)

All this was my roundabout way of saying that I believe there has always been, and will continue to be, a need for people who are able to reason through complex ideas and express them in simple ways. The shape of the job may change as various parts like writing code are automated away, but the job of the software engineer is so much more than that. Those who understand this and hone the right skills will continue to be in demand.

Of course, it doesn't feel that way looking at the job market right now. There are two uncomfortable truths that we must face to understand this. Firstly, engineers are realizing that quality is not always king. Sometimes, corporates prefer to pay for the quick and dirty vibe-coded slop that gets 80% of the job done for 20% of the cost. An engineer like myself can scream about the long-term perils of tech debt and inordinately complex systems until the cows come home, but a leader who's just thinking about the earnings call next quarter will not care.

Similarly when it comes to headcounts, AI has resulted in companies being able to accomplish more work with less engineers than before - and any person responsible for the budget of a company will immediately see an opportunity to cut costs without affecting the forecasted work output. There is no denying the impact and pain unleashed on employees due to their decisions, but at the end of the day, it is the cold mathematical truth driving the capitalist world we were born into.

So that is the sad state of things today. Jobs and vacancies do exist, but in less numbers. Besides, freshers are now competing directly with more experienced engineers who lost their previous roles, so the competition is very intense.
