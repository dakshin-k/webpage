In the cybersecurity world, the phrase 'script kiddie' is used to describe someone who, to put it simply, doesn't know what they are doing. Think someone googling "how to find my neighbour's WiFi password" and blindly following the instructions on whatever page they land on. They might or might not succeed - at this point it is more a question of luck than of skill - but since they didn't do it themselves and don't know how to do so, most professionals in the field tend to treat them with a bit of contempt.

The rise of LLMs has unleashed the same behaviour in the field of software engineering, only at a much grander scale. There are literal hordes of individuals churning out apps and websites of unimaginable varieties - and yet they all have something missing. A button that doesn't let you click it, a textbox that's invisible on mobile but seen on a desktop, or a page that flat out doesn't do as advertised. Each time the author fixes one thing, something seemingly unrelated breaks, or the UI is fixed but the app still doesn't work. The author usually is aware of these shortcomings, but somehow, each update is never quite what they had in mind. Any experienced professional immediately recognizes these symptoms: it is a result of the author not being 'in sync' with the code - they don't know what part of the code does what, how it does it and what other parts it talks to. Traditionally, every engineer began in this exact place on day one, with zero knowledge and built up their understanding by making small changes to the code and observing the results. This took a lot of time and effort, but it was effective. Today, LLMs are taking away the need to write code, leaving those without this context in their heads to just keep trying different variations of the same prompt in the hope that something changes.

I call these individuals the prompt monkeys.

It's important to understand that everybody begins their journey as a prompt monkey. The difference between pre-LLM times to now, is that with time and practice, people used to gain a better understanding of their code and their tech, eventually pulling themselves up to a higher level of skill. But when AI writes all the code for us, how do we get better?

But this is just one of the existential questions facing freshers today. There's a ton more: How do I even get a job in the first place? If I do manage to find a job, how do I hold on to it, with AI threatening to replace me everyday? What if I don't want to be a prompt monkey forever? How do I learn and grow in this role, making a career out of it for the next few decades? Most importantly, how do I not lose myself along the way, burning out from the stress and immense workload?
